# Chapter 3 --- Some Analytical Techniques

## Core idea of the chapter

Most substances obtained from nature or prepared in a laboratory are not
automatically pure. Separation and purification techniques work by
exploiting **differences in physical properties** of the components of a
mixture.

A useful way to approach almost every method in this chapter is:

> **Ask: "In what physical property are these substances sufficiently
> different?"**

Examples: - solubility → filtration / crystallization / solvent
extraction - change of solubility with temperature → crystallization /
fractional crystallization - volatility or boiling point →
distillation - different affinities for stationary and mobile phases →
chromatography

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 1. Filtration

Filtration separates an **insoluble solid** from a liquid.

Example: sand + water.

-   **Residue:** material left on the filter paper.
-   **Filtrate:** liquid that passes through the filter paper.

### Example: separating salt and sand

1.  Add water.
2.  Salt dissolves; sand does not.
3.  Filter the mixture.
    -   sand → residue
    -   salt solution → filtrate
4.  Remove the water to recover salt.

Two physical differences are being exploited: 1. salt and sand have
different solubilities in water; 2. water is volatile while salt is
essentially nonvolatile under these conditions.

## Filtration under suction

A vacuum pump reduces the pressure inside the receiving flask. The
pressure difference helps force liquid through the filter paper.

Advantages: - faster than gravity filtration; - removes mother liquor
from crystals efficiently; - leaves collected crystals relatively less
wet.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 2. Crystallization

Crystallization is used to purify a solid containing impurities.

The central idea is that the desired substance is usually:

-   highly soluble in a hot solvent;
-   much less soluble in the same solvent when cold.

A typical sequence is:

1.  Dissolve the impure solid in a **minimum quantity of hot solvent**.
2.  Filter the hot solution to remove insoluble impurities.
3.  Cool the filtrate.
4.  The desired substance crystallizes.
5.  Soluble impurities ideally remain in solution.
6.  Filter the crystals.

The liquid remaining after crystals have been removed is called the
**mother liquor**.

## Why use the minimum amount of hot solvent?

Suppose 100 mL water dissolves:

-   50 g of A at 80 °C
-   10 g of A at 20 °C

To dissolve 10 g A at 80 °C, only:

\[ 100`\times`{=tex}`\frac{10}{50}`{=tex}=20`\text{ mL}`{=tex} \]

is required.

At 20 °C, 20 mL can retain:

\[ 10`\times`{=tex}`\frac{20}{100}`{=tex}=2`\text{ g}`{=tex} \]

Therefore:

\[ 10-2=8`\text{ g}`{=tex} \]

can crystallize.

If we unnecessarily used 100 mL water, all 10 g could remain dissolved
even at 20 °C, giving essentially no crystals.

Hence:

> **Too much solvent → more desired compound remains dissolved after
> cooling → lower recovery.**

A useful approximate mental model is:

\[ `\text{crystal yield}`{=tex} `\approx`{=tex}
`\text{amount dissolved hot}`{=tex} -
`\text{amount remaining soluble cold}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 3. Fractional Crystallization

Fractional crystallization separates two or more **soluble solids** by
exploiting sufficiently different solubility behavior.

Suppose at 20 °C, in 100 mL water:

-   A: solubility = 5 g
-   B: solubility = 40 g

A hot solution contains:

-   30 g A
-   30 g B

After cooling to 20 °C:

-   only 5 g A can remain dissolved → 25 g A crystallizes;
-   all 30 g B can remain dissolved.

The mother liquor therefore contains approximately:

-   5 g A
-   30 g B

After filtering A, the mother liquor can be **concentrated by
evaporating solvent** and cooled again so that B can crystallize.

Typical sequence:

\[ `\text{dissolve}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{cool}`{=tex}
`\rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{crystallize A}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{filter}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{concentrate mother liquor}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{cool}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{crystallize B}`{=tex} \]

## Critique of the textbook definition

The textbook defines fractional crystallization using substances having
widely different solubilities in the same solvent **"at room
temperature."**

This is potentially misleading because the useful distinction is not
merely that their solubilities differ at room temperature. The process
depends on choosing conditions---commonly changing temperature and/or
concentration---under which one component crystallizes preferentially
while another remains largely dissolved.

The phrase "separated by crystallization" also does not explain what
makes the process *fractional*.

### Improved definition

> **Fractional crystallization is a method for separating a mixture of
> soluble solids by exploiting differences in their solubilities,
> usually their differing dependence on temperature, so that under
> appropriately chosen conditions one component crystallizes
> preferentially while the other component(s) remain largely dissolved
> in the mother liquor.**

Shorter version:

> **Fractional crystallization is the separation of two or more soluble
> solids by exploiting differences in their solubilities so that they
> crystallize preferentially under different conditions.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 4. Distillation

Distillation exploits differences in **volatility / boiling point**.

The basic sequence is:

\[ `\text{liquid}`{=tex} `\xrightarrow{\text{heating}}`{=tex}
`\text{vapour}`{=tex} `\xrightarrow{\text{cooling}}`{=tex}
`\text{liquid distillate}`{=tex} \]

A **condenser** cools the vapour and converts it back into liquid.

## Simple distillation

Useful when: - separating a volatile liquid from nonvolatile impurities;
or - separating liquids whose boiling points differ sufficiently.

Example: recovering pure water from salt water.

The salt remains in the flask while water vaporizes, enters the
condenser, condenses and is collected.

## Important subtlety

For a mixture of two volatile liquids A and B, it is generally incorrect
to imagine that at A's boiling point **only A enters the vapour**.

Both substances normally contribute to the vapour.

If A is more volatile:

\[ `\text{vapour is richer in A than the original liquid}`{=tex} \]

This enrichment is the basis of fractional distillation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 5. Fractional Distillation

Fractional distillation is useful when volatile liquids have relatively
close boiling points.

Imagine a 50:50 mixture of A and B where A is more volatile.

The first vapour may be richer in A:

\[ 50%A+50%B `\rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{vapour richer in A}`{=tex} \]

Condensing and vaporizing this enriched mixture again produces vapour
still richer in A.

Conceptually:

\[ A+B `\rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{richer in A}`{=tex}
`\rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{still richer in A}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{mostly A}`{=tex} \]

A **fractionating column** performs many repeated microscopic
vaporization--condensation steps inside one apparatus.

Useful mental model:

> **A fractionating column is like performing many small distillations
> automatically.**

The more volatile component preferentially enriches the rising vapour.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 6. Distillation under Reduced Pressure

Some liquids: - have very high boiling points; or - decompose before
reaching their normal boiling points.

Boiling occurs when:

\[ P\_{`\text{vapour}`{=tex}}(T)=P\_{`\text{external}`{=tex}} \]

Reducing external pressure lowers the vapour pressure that the liquid
must reach before boiling begins.

Therefore:

\[ `\downarrow `{=tex}P\_{`\text{external}`{=tex}} `\Rightarrow`{=tex}
`\downarrow `{=tex}T\_{`\text{boiling}`{=tex}} \]

This allows a substance to be distilled at a temperature below its
normal boiling point and may prevent decomposition.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. Boiling Point, Altitude and Pressure Cookers

Water does not universally boil at 100 °C.

More accurately:

> **Water boils at approximately 100 °C when the external pressure is
> approximately 1 atm.**

At high altitude:

\[ `\text{higher altitude}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{lower atmospheric pressure}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{lower boiling point}`{=tex} \]

Food immersed in boiling water is therefore cooked at a lower
temperature and generally cooks more slowly.

A pressure cooker does the opposite:

\[ `\text{higher pressure}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{higher boiling point}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{higher cooking temperature}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{faster cooking}`{=tex} \]

Vacuum distillation deliberately creates the same general pressure
effect as going to high altitude:

\[ `\text{reduce external pressure}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{reduce boiling point}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 8. What Happens When Salt Is Added to Boiling Water?

Dissolved salt lowers the **vapour pressure of water at a given
temperature**.

This must not be confused with lowering **external pressure**.

The boiling condition remains:

\[ P\_{`\text{vapour}`{=tex}}=P\_{`\text{external}`{=tex}} \]

### Lowering external pressure

The target pressure becomes easier to reach:

\[ `\downarrow `{=tex}P\_{`\text{external}`{=tex}} `\Rightarrow`{=tex}
`\downarrow `{=tex}T_b \]

### Lowering the liquid's vapour pressure at a given temperature

External pressure remains unchanged, but the solution now needs a higher
temperature to produce enough vapour pressure:

\[ `\downarrow `{=tex}P\_{`\text{vapour at given }`{=tex}T}
`\Rightarrow`{=tex} `\uparrow `{=tex}T_b \]

Thus adding a nonvolatile solute such as salt causes **boiling-point
elevation**.

During prolonged boiling of salt water: - mainly water escapes as
vapour; - salt concentration increases; - the boiling point can rise
further; - sufficiently concentrated salt may eventually crystallize.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 9. Condenser Cooling Water

Cooling water is conventionally supplied:

\[ `\boxed{\text{IN at bottom, OUT at top}}`{=tex} \]

Why?

Water entering from below fills the jacket upward and pushes air toward
the upper outlet. This makes complete filling of the cooling jacket
reliable.

If water enters from the top, especially at modest flow rates, it can
flow downward toward the outlet while leaving air pockets.

Important qualification:

> Top-entry **can** fill the jacket if the flow rate and geometry permit
> it. Bottom-entry is preferred because it naturally and reliably
> displaces air upward and keeps the cooling surface immersed.

In suitable condenser arrangements, opposite flow directions of
vapour/condensate and cooling water can also provide **counter-current
heat exchange**, but that is a secondary consideration here.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 10. Solvent Extraction

Suppose substance A is dissolved in water but is much more soluble in
another liquid that:

-   is immiscible with water;
-   forms a separate layer.

After shaking:

\[ A\_{`\text{water}`{=tex}} `\rightleftharpoons`{=tex}
A\_{`\text{organic solvent}`{=tex}} \]

A distributes itself between the two phases, but more enters the solvent
in which it is more soluble.

The two liquid layers are separated, typically with a **separating
funnel**---not by filtration.

The organic solvent can then be removed, for example by distillation, to
recover the solute.

## Why several small extractions can outperform one large extraction

Each extraction removes a **fraction of the solute that remains**.

Illustrative example: suppose each fresh extraction removes 60% of the A
currently remaining in water.

Starting with 100 units:

\[
100`\rightarrow40`{=tex}`\rightarrow16`{=tex}`\rightarrow6.4`{=tex}`\rightarrow2.56`{=tex}
\]

Repeated portions of fresh solvent repeatedly establish a new
distribution equilibrium and can therefore extract more solute overall.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 11. Chromatography

Chromatography separates components because different substances move at
different rates under the influence of a **mobile phase** while
interacting with a **stationary phase**.

The terminology can be confusing.

Here, "phase" does **not** mean sequential stages such as:

\[
`\text{stationary phase}`{=tex}`\rightarrow`{=tex}`\text{mobile phase}`{=tex}
\]

The stationary and mobile phases exist simultaneously and are usually
different parts/materials of the chromatographic system.

Useful mental replacements:

> **Stationary phase = holder**\
> **Mobile phase = carrier**

## Mobile phase

The mobile phase **moves** through or across the stationary phase and
carries the mixture.

## Stationary phase

The stationary phase **remains in place** and interacts with components
of the mixture.

If A interacts strongly with the stationary phase, it spends more time
being retained.

If B prefers the mobile phase, it spends more time moving.

Therefore:

\[ `\text{greater affinity for mobile phase}`{=tex} `\Rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{travels farther/faster}`{=tex} \]

\[ `\text{greater affinity for stationary phase}`{=tex}
`\Rightarrow`{=tex} `\text{travels less/slower}`{=tex} \]

For now, this conceptual understanding is sufficient; deeper
chromatography can be revisited later when polarity, intermolecular
forces and equilibria are better developed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 12. Adsorption Chromatography

Here the stationary phase is a **solid**, commonly silica gel or
alumina.

Components repeatedly: - travel with the mobile solvent; - adsorb onto
the stationary surface; - desorb and move again.

Different substances adsorb to different extents.

A strongly adsorbed compound is retained longer.

A weakly adsorbed compound moves faster and may leave the column first.

## Adsorption vs absorption

-   **Adsorption:** accumulation/sticking **on a surface**.
-   **Absorption:** penetration **into the bulk** of a material.

## Column chromatography

A column is packed with a stationary solid phase. Solvent moves through
it.

Different components form bands and leave the column at different times
according to how strongly they are adsorbed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Thin-Layer Chromatography (TLC)

A thin layer of silica gel or alumina on a plate acts as the stationary
phase.

A small sample spot is placed near one end. The plate is placed in a
solvent with the spot **above the solvent level**.

The solvent rises and carries components with it.

Why must the sample spot remain above the solvent reservoir?

If submerged, the sample may dissolve directly into the bulk solvent
reservoir and be lost rather than beginning from a well-defined position
on the plate.

## Retention factor, (R_f)

\[ R_f= `\frac{\text{distance travelled by solute from baseline}}`{=tex}
{`\text{distance travelled by solvent front from baseline}`{=tex}} \]

Example:

-   solute travels 9 cm;
-   solvent front travels 12 cm.

\[ R_f=`\frac{9}{12}`{=tex}=0.75 \]

Normally:

\[ 0`\le `{=tex}R_f`\le1`{=tex} \]

A relatively high (R_f) means the compound travelled relatively far with
the mobile phase under those conditions.

A relatively low (R_f) means it was retained more strongly.

Important:

> **(R_f) is not a universal intrinsic constant of a compound.** It
> depends on the stationary phase, mobile phase and experimental
> conditions.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. Partition Chromatography

In the textbook's treatment, both stationary and mobile phases are
liquids.

Components separate through repeated **partitioning** between the two
phases.

## Paper chromatography

The textbook describes water trapped in cellulose fibres of the paper as
the stationary liquid phase.

The mobile solvent rises through the paper by capillary action.

Components repeatedly distribute between:

\[ `\text{stationary water}`{=tex} `\rightleftharpoons`{=tex}
`\text{solute}`{=tex} `\rightleftharpoons`{=tex}
`\text{mobile solvent}`{=tex} \]

Substances that preferentially associate with the mobile phase move
farther.

Substances preferentially retained by the stationary phase move less.

Again, the sample spot must remain above the solvent reservoir so it is
carried upward by the advancing solvent front rather than dissolving
directly into the reservoir.

# 15. Tangent --- Why Do Crystals Dissolve in Water?

A crystal does not literally "disappear." Its particles become dispersed
on a molecular/ionic scale throughout the solvent.

For an ionic solid such as NaCl:

\[ `\mathrm{NaCl(s)\rightarrow Na^+(aq)+Cl^-(aq)}`{=tex} \]

The ions in the crystal are strongly attracted to one another.

Water is polar:

-   oxygen side: partial negative charge;
-   hydrogen side: partial positive charge.

Water molecules form **ion--dipole interactions** with the ions: -
oxygen atoms orient toward positive ions such as Na⁺; - hydrogen atoms
orient toward negative ions such as Cl⁻.

Water molecules surround and stabilize separated ions. This is
**hydration**.

Thus dissolution involves competition between: - interactions holding
particles in the crystal; - interactions between those particles and
water; - additional thermodynamic effects such as entropy.

The notation `(aq)` therefore does not mean a naked ion floating alone.
It indicates a species solvated by water.
